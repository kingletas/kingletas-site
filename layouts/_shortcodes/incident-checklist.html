The first fifteen minutes

  1. An incident nobody has declared is several people fixing different things in private.

  2. Without one person deciding, nothing gets decided.

  3. Memory after an incident is wrong; the timeline is the record.

  4. Severity decides who gets woken and how often people hear from you.

  5. A running fault keeps adding harm while you diagnose it.

  6. People decide what to do from updates; silence makes them guess.

  7. Runbooks hold the steps nobody remembers at 3 a.m.

  8. A missed update reads as "they have lost control".

Who holds which role

On a small team one person holds several roles. Say out loud which ones, so nobody assumes someone else has them.

Decides, coordinates, sets severity, calls rollback or failover, owns the updates. Doesn't fix things while commanding.

Writes the timeline: every step, time, finding and decision, and who made it.

The commander, until the incident is big enough to need a second person. Nothing unconfirmed goes outside.

Makes the business calls: accept lost orders, take the site down, fail over.

Everyone else is a responder: investigate and fix, saying each step out loud, and don't change production without telling the commander.

Severity, from what users cannot do

A starting point to adapt, not a standard. Raise severity freely and lower it carefully: starting high and stepping down costs a few minutes; starting low and discovering it was the top level costs the incident.

The first five questions

Ask them out loud, in this order, and put the answers in the timeline.

The shape of an update

What happened.
What people are seeing.
What we know.
What we do not know yet.
What we are doing.
When the next update comes.

Confirmed and suspected never share a sentence: "Checkout is failing for card payments. We think it started with this morning's deploy and are checking." Promise the next update, not a fix time, and keep it.
